Most Easter eggs are made to be hidden in the grass and found by a child before breakfast. Vegreville, Alberta built one to be found from the highway.

It stands tilted over Elks/Kinsmen Park at the east end of town, gold, bronze, black, and white, patterned like a jewel and sized like a small building. The town bills it as the world's largest pysanka, and after fifty years on the skyline, it has become the signature of a community that decided its heritage deserved to be seen from a moving car.

## What Is a Pysanka?

A pysanka is a Ukrainian Easter egg, decorated with traditional Ukrainian folk designs using a wax-resist method. The artist writes the pattern onto the shell in hot wax with a fine stylus, dyes the egg, then writes again, building the design in layers until the wax comes off and the finished pattern appears, bright and exact.

Stars, triangles, bands, and crosses carry meanings handed down through generations, and no two eggs are ever quite the same. It is slow, patient, kitchen table work, traditionally done by hand, one egg at a time, often by somebody's grandmother, usually faster and steadier than you can manage.

Vegreville's version simply does that work at highway scale. The same stars and triangles that fit on a hen's egg here cover an aluminum shell 25.7 feet long, 18 feet wide, and 31 feet high.

## How Do You Build an Egg Three Storeys Tall?

Carefully, and with a computer, at a time when that was still a startling sentence. The sculpture was designed by Paul Maxym Sembaliuk, a Canadian artist of Ukrainian descent born and raised in the Willingdon and Vegreville area, together with Professor Ron Resch, a computer scientist at the University of Utah.

An egg is a trickier shape than it looks. It is not a sphere, not an oval, and it refuses to be described by any simple formula, which is why the Vegreville Pysanka is recognized as the first computer modeling of an egg, and as an achievement of nine mathematical, architectural, and engineering firsts. The finished shell is an immense jigsaw puzzle: 524 star patterns, 2,208 equilateral triangles, 3,512 visible facets, 6,978 nuts and bolts, and 177 internal struts, all in aluminum.

Then comes the party trick. The whole egg is mounted so it can turn in the wind like a weathervane, slowly presenting a different patterned face to the park. Stand there long enough on a breezy day and the monument quietly rearranges itself.

## Why an Egg, and Why the Mounties?

In 1973, the Government of Alberta established the Alberta Century Celebrations Committee to coordinate the centennial celebrations of the Royal Canadian Mounted Police, and the committee distributed funds to several Alberta municipalities, Vegreville among them. With those funds, the Vegreville and District Chamber of Commerce chose to build the Pysanka, to symbolize the town's rich Ukrainian culture and the peace and security the RCMP offered the area's pioneers and their descendants.

It was dedicated in 1975, and it has been drawing travellers off the Yellowhead Highway ever since. In 1978 it received the most distinguished roadside review in the country: Queen Elizabeth II and Prince Philip, the Duke of Edinburgh, came to Vegreville to see it for themselves.

> Any town can put its name on a sign. Vegreville wrote its name in geometry, thirty-one feet up, and let the wind turn the pages.

## Worth the Stop

The Town of Vegreville says the Pysanka is available for viewing 365 days a year, and the park around it makes the stop easy: green space, a pond, room for kids to run and for travellers to stretch after the drive east from Edmonton. Make it part of the great Ukrainian food loop of east-central Alberta, with Glendon's giant perogy on its fork and Mundare's giant sausage keeping watch not far away.

Stand underneath it at golden hour, when the gold and bronze panels catch the low sun and the whole egg seems lit from inside its own pattern. A pysanka is traditionally a gift, a small decorated wish for life and good fortune. Vegreville just made the wish big enough for the whole highway to read.
