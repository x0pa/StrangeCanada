Every Manitoba town has a rink. In most of them, the rink is where winter happens: the bonspiels, the canteen coffee, the kids who learn to sweep before they learn to drive. Arborg, in Manitoba's Interlake, has all of that too.

Then, in 2005, Arborg put its feelings about curling outside the rink, at a scale you can see from the road.

## How Big Is the World's Largest Curling Rock?

The monument measures 4.2 metres across — about 13.8 feet — and 2.1 metres tall, raised up on white supports outside the Arborg Curling Club, at the town's recreation complex off Ingolfs Street. Travel Manitoba describes it as towering at over 12 feet from its base. Arborg claims it plainly as the world's largest curling rock, and standing under it, looking up at a stone wide enough to park a small car on, the claim is easy to enjoy.

For scale, consider the real thing. A regulation curling rock is a disc of granite you can wrap both hands around, heavy enough to respect and small enough to slide down a sheet of ice with one arm. Arborg's version takes that familiar shape — the low, wide disc, the single handle on top — and blows it up until it stops being sports equipment and becomes architecture.

## Why Did Arborg Build It?

Not for tourists, originally. For its own kids.

The rock was raised in 2005 to pay tribute to two local high school curling teams who won provincial high school championship titles. The Town of Arborg tells the story with justified pride: two local teams made it to the provincials, and one of them went on to win the nationals. In a province that takes curling personally, a national title won by teenagers from a town of Arborg's size is the kind of thing you commemorate properly.

So the town commemorated it the Manitoba way: permanently, outdoors, and much larger than strictly necessary. A plaque at the base of the monument commemorates past provincial champions, turning a photo stop into a small hall of fame with no walls.

## What Is It Actually Made Of?

Here is the monument's best secret: the world's largest curling rock is not made of rock.

A real curling stone is solid granite. Arborg's giant is built of steel, foam, and fibreglass, with most of its weight — reported at about 3,000 pounds — sitting in the steel supports that hold it aloft. That is not a cheat; it is engineering. A solid granite rock at this scale would be a quarrying project, not a town monument. Steel and fibreglass gave Arborg the silhouette it wanted at a weight its supports could carry, through every Manitoba winter since.

The effect, in person, is faithful. The pale grey body, the smooth curved profile, and the bright red handle read instantly, even from across the parking lot: that is a curling rock, mid-delivery to nowhere, frozen at the exact moment before the sweepers take over.

> Two school teams won, and a town built them a rock too big to throw. That is not excess. That is a thank-you note, written at scale.

## Worth the Stop

Arborg's giant works because it is honest about what it celebrates. It is not a rock that happens to be big. It is a trophy that happens to be a rock — a town looking at what its young people did on the ice and deciding the achievement deserved to be visible from the highway.

Pull in off Ingolfs Street, read the plaque, and take the photo. Somewhere inside the rink behind it, the next team is practising.
