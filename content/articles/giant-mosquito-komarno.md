Most towns spend their summers at war with the mosquito. They spray for it, swat at it, and apologize for it to visitors. Komarno, Manitoba did something else entirely.

It built one a monument.

## How Big Is Komarno's Giant Mosquito?

Big enough to stop traffic on a side road in the Interlake. The statue measures about 4.6 metres — 15 feet — long, with a wingspan reported at close to 17 feet: long legs, a long needle of a proboscis, and wide veined wings, all of it raised above a stone base in the village, west on PR 229 off Highway 8, about 70 kilometres north of Winnipeg.

Komarno claims it plainly as the world's largest mosquito, and the community has just as proudly claimed the mosquito capital title that goes with it. Other towns compete to be capitals of things people love: lobster, curling, fiddles. Komarno looked at the insect every Manitoban complains about from June to September and claimed the crown nobody else was reaching for.

For scale, consider the real thing. A living Manitoba mosquito is a few millimetres of whine and bad intentions, small enough to land on your arm unnoticed until it is too late. Komarno's version is roughly a thousand times that ambition, built at a size where the whine would be a foghorn and the bite a civic emergency. Mercifully, it has never bitten anyone. It just stands there, enormous and unbothered, while visitors pose underneath it and tell the camera the old prairie joke: in Manitoba, this is only a medium one.

## Why Would a Town Build a Mosquito?

Because the mosquito was already in the name.

Komarno is a Ukrainian word that, as the Manitoba Historical Society puts it, means "lots of mosquitos." The community's settlers named the place honestly, after the clouds of insects that rise off the Interlake's wet prairie every summer, and a century later the village decided honesty deserved a statue.

The Manitoba Historical Society records that the monument, built of stone and metal, was designed and built between 1984 and 1986 by artist Marlene Magnusson Hourd, to commemorate the name of the community. It was unveiled in October 1986 — sensibly, after mosquito season, so the real ones could not attend and upstage it.

## A Mosquito That Tells the Wind

Here is the detail that lifts Komarno's giant above the ordinary run of prairie statues: it works for a living. The mosquito is mounted as a weathervane, turning on its base to face into the wind, so the insect that summer travellers watch for is also the one that tells local farmers which way the weather is moving.

That makes every visit slightly different. One day the proboscis points down Highway 8 toward Winnipeg; the next, it has swung around to face the fields. It is, in the best prairie tradition, a joke that is also genuinely useful — a landmark, a weather instrument, and a warning, all in one insect.

> Every other town warns you about its mosquitos. Komarno introduces you to theirs, by name, at fifteen feet long.

## Worth the Stop

There is a particular Manitoba genius in Komarno's giant. The Interlake summer mosquito is not a branding opportunity anyone would choose; it chose them, it is in the very name of the place, and the village's answer was not denial but scale. If you are going to be named for lots of mosquitos, you may as well have the biggest one.

Pull off Highway 8, follow PR 229 west, and check which way it is pointing. Whatever direction the wind has given it, it is facing the same fact Komarno has faced cheerfully since 1986: the mosquitos were here first, and the town has made its peace with them — permanently, in stone and metal, at a size nobody can swat.
